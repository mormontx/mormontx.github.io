## The Cage

A rubric is a cage with good intentions. It is built to protect the student from arbitrariness and the teacher from the accusation of it, and for most assignments it does both. The trouble begins when the assignment is a question that is supposed to buckle. A rubric is load-bearing only for problems that have already been solved; it cannot hold a problem that is still deciding what it is.

I learned this the year I wrote a brief too short on purpose: a single paragraph, no criteria, no word count, no model answer. The first cohort declared it broken, and I agreed. What they could not see was that the brokenness was the brief. An earlier version of the same exercise had carried a rubric, and its answers had varied only in wording. When I withdrew the rubric, the answers varied in kind — and the room had to negotiate what the question even meant.

## What a Rubric Does

A rubric pre-loads the decisions a student is supposed to make. It announces where the weight sits, so the student aims there, and so the interesting failure never happens. The under-specified brief works in the opposite direction: it withholds the criteria so the student must state an assumption and defend it. That is the load. The rubric, however humane, removes the load and calls the result clarity.

<aside class="sidenote">Dewey: an aim is a way of ordering activity, not a fixed end. A rubric fixes the end and quietly disables the ordering.</aside>

None of this means a rubric is wrong. It means it is a tool with a hidden cost, and the cost is paid precisely where the question is most alive. A rubric is an answer to the problem of fairness. It is not an answer to the problem of thought.

## Fairness Is Not Legibility

The usual defense of the rubric is that it is fair, and the defense deserves a better answer than the one I used to give. A rubric is not fair; it is legible. It makes a judgment reproducible, so that two readers land near the same number, and it makes that reproducibility visible to the student in advance. Legibility is a real good — it lets a student plan and a department audit — but it is not the same good as fairness. Two students can earn the same row on the grid for opposite reasons: one for having genuinely grasped the material, the other for having aimed precisely at the grid and nothing else. The number cannot tell them apart, because the number was never asked to.

This is the part I used to miss. I assumed that if the criteria were clear, the grade would be just. Withdrawing the rubric taught me that fairness lives one level down: in whether the student had a real chance to decide what the work was for. A criterion handed over in advance has already made that decision on their behalf. Fairness, if the word still has work to do, is the chance to be wrong about the question and to be met where the error is.

## The Repair

I still use rubrics, for the assignments that are meant to converge. For the ones meant to buckle, I withhold them until the end, and sometimes not even then. The students draft their own criteria, argue over the weighting, and discover that the argument is the assignment. The brief I wrote is still too short, and the rubric, when it finally appears, is written by the class. That is the only cage I am willing to keep: one the students build themselves, and can leave when the question needs them to.
