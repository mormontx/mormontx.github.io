The Moment

I wrote the assignment too short on purpose, and the first cohort hated it. It was a single paragraph: a question about what a question is for, with no rubric, no word count, no model answer, and no place to hide. On the first afternoon a student raised the page between two fingers and said, “This is broken.” The room agreed, and the agreement was loud. I agreed with them, and asked what we should do about it. I had built the thing to fail on purpose, and it was failing exactly on schedule, though none of us could say so yet.

For three weeks the prompt did exactly what I had built it to do: it buckled. Every attempt to answer it exposed a different assumption about what was being asked. One group read it as a philosophy exercise, another as a design brief, a third as a test of our patience. The forum filled with competing readings, each one correcting the one before it, each correction opening a new crack in what we thought the assignment meant. I stopped moderating and let the threads argue; the loudest voices had to explain themselves to the quietest. Nobody was satisfied, which meant everybody was working.

Dewey: an aim is not a fixed end but a way of ordering activity; the prompt is the problem the students must reconstruct.

The turn came in week six. I stopped seeing the phrase “this is broken” and started seeing repair. A student proposed that we replace the prompt with a new one, written by the class. Two others objected, not to the idea but to the wording. Someone opened a thread titled “minimum viable assignment” and, without my help, they began to draft criteria they could all live with — then argued about who got to grade the argument itself. I watched the document fork and rejoin over three nights, every edit a small act of load-bearing.

Execution note: the forum was asynchronous Markdown, so every repair left a visible diff. The negotiation was the syllabus.

By week fourteen the cohort submitted a revised brief as their final act, and it was worse than mine in the best possible way: longer, less certain, and full of the seams where they had patched it. The prompt never became stable. It stayed load-bearing precisely because it kept almost falling down, and the students had learned to catch it — or, better, to rebuild it before it hit the floor.

Why It Stayed

This structure stays because it showed me that a broken prompt is not a failure of design but a working load. When the assignment stops specifying, the students start deciding. I return to it whenever a rubric begins to look like a cage.

Blueprint / Constraints · Failure Points · Iterations +

Constraints

  1. The prompt may not define its own grading criteria
  2. Every answer must state the assumption it chose to load
  3. A revision to the prompt counts as a completed assignment

Failure Points

  • The cohort requests a 'real' prompt and stops negotiating
  • One strong reading becomes canonical and freezes the structure

Iterations

2022

a fully specified brief — answers varied only in wording

2023

criteria stripped; the prompt is left to buckle

2024

students inherit the brief before the first meeting
