The Moment

I stopped grading drafts the year I noticed where the students were looking. They flipped to the last page for the number and read nothing else. So I put away the red pen and, with a pencil, wrote only questions in the margins: Why here? What would this paragraph say if it could contradict you? Who is speaking in this sentence — you, or the author you are quoting?

The first drafts came back uneasy. A question in the margin is a strange object: it neither praises nor condemns, and students are not sure what to do with a reader who only asks. One of them wrote to me after class, half joking, that a grade would have been easier to ignore. I told her that was the point, and that I would not be providing an easier thing. A margin question, I said, is like a door left open; a grade is a door shut behind you. She asked how she was supposed to answer a question with no right answer, and I told her the same way she would answer a letter.

Vygotsky: development happens in the space between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with a more capable other. The margin is that space.

The correspondence began without my permission. In the next draft, a student had answered my margin question in her own margin, in smaller handwriting, as if replying to a letter. Then she asked me a question back. I answered it in the next round, and by the third draft we were no longer performing feedback but exchanging it — two readers of the same text, neither of us entirely in charge. Her handwriting got smaller, then steadier, the way a voice does when it stops asking permission.

Execution note: pencil, not pen. Questions must stay open to being answered, argued with, or crossed out.

By the end of the seminar the margins were the liveliest part of the page. Some students had started annotating one another’s work before I touched it, and the margins had begun to speak in more than two voices. What I had designed as a single correspondence had become a network. A thirty-first margin opened for me, and I took my place in it as one reader among many. The grade, when it finally came, was almost an afterthought — a number standing at the end of a conversation it had not been invited to.

Why It Stayed

This structure stays because it converted evaluation into correspondence. The margin is narrow, but it is wide enough for two voices. When I write a question now, I write it the way I would write to a colleague: expecting an answer, and prepared to be changed by it.

Blueprint

Constraints · Failure Points · Iterations +

Constraints

  1. Every comment must take the form of a question
  2. No grade or score may appear on a draft
  3. The margin is the only place feedback may live

Failure Points

  • A question reads as a disguised correction and the student stops answering
  • The teacher's hand crowds the margin and silences the student's

Iterations

2023

grades on drafts — students read only the number

2024

margin questions replace verdicts; replies begin

2025

students annotate one another's margins
