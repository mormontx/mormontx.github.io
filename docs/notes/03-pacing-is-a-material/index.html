Pacing Is a Material

Three minutes of timed silence, and the instructor's own discomfort as part of the scaffold.

The Interval

The rule was simple and I hated enforcing it: after every contribution, three minutes of silence. Timed, no exceptions, no rescue. The first interval was the longest three minutes I have spent in a classroom. Two students studied the table. One checked the clock twice. Another looked at me as if I had taken the air out of the room, and I sat with my hands flat on the table, trying to look as though the discomfort were part of the plan — because it was.

What I had not anticipated was that my own discomfort would become load-bearing. The silence was hard for the students, but it was harder for me. I am the one who fills pauses; it is half the job. Every three minutes I felt the old reflex rise — to soften, to explain, to narrate the pause until it stopped being a pause. If I had indulged it, the scaffold would have collapsed into the very thing it was meant to replace. My restraint, not my instruction, was the material the room learned from.

hooks: silence is not absence but a different kind of presence. The room learns to dwell in it before it thinks in it.

The Turn

The change arrived in the fourth week, quietly, the way these things do — in a voice that had not spoken all term. A student waited out her three minutes and then read a paragraph she had written during the gap. It was finished. Not a sketch, not a hedge, but a whole idea with its edges intact. The fast speakers had learned to stop filling the pause, and the slow thinkers had learned the pause was theirs.

By the sixth week the interval had shortened without anyone’s permission. The room kept its own time now, and nobody looked at the clock. Pacing, I understood, is not a technique but a material: it can be laid too thin, it can bear weight, and it can be handled badly until the room learns to handle it itself.

What Stayed

I keep the silence because it taught me what I cannot teach by speaking: that the gaps between utterances do as much work as the utterances themselves. The bell still sits on my desk. Some weeks I do not use it. Some weeks I need it more than the students do.
